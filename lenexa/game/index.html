The Lantern Room

Five situations. Three ways through each one. Nothing you decide is written down anywhere — that is the point of the room.

Wireframe — early prototype. Art, audio and the full scenario set are not built yet. What you are looking at is the decision loop, the reflective tone, the teacher view and the no-data guarantee, built far enough to actually play. Everything else is deliberately unfinished so it can be shaped by the officer and the classroom before a line of production work is done.

Begin in the Lantern Room

A short, playable scenario game for ages 11–14, built to be run by a guest teacher in front of a class — or played alone, quietly, by one student. Each situation is one a middle-schooler already recognises: a message from a stranger, a group chat turning on someone, a request for a photo, a password that got out, a friend left outside the circle.

There is no score, no timer and no buzzer for a wrong answer. Every choice gets an honest consequence and a name for the principle it exercised. A class can argue about it afterwards. That argument is the lesson.

Teacher view — running this in a classroom

Suggested format: project the screen, read the situation aloud, take a show of hands on all three choices before revealing any consequence, then click the one the room chose. The discussion prompt for each scenario appears in this same panel while Teacher view is on.

  • Running time: roughly 12–18 minutes for the five scenarios, plus discussion.
  • Nothing here produces a record about any student — no sign-in, no names typed, nothing stored. Say that out loud at the start; for some children it is the first time they have heard it.
  • If a student discloses something real during discussion, that leaves the game and follows your school's existing safeguarding process. This wireframe deliberately contains no reporting mechanism.

        Teacher view — closing discussion

        • Which of the five was hardest to agree on as a room? Hard usually means realistic.
        • Did anyone change their mind after seeing a consequence? What changed it?
        • Ask: who is the adult you would actually go to? Not who you should go to — who you would. If the honest answer is nobody, that is worth knowing.
        • Close on the no-data panel below. Ask the room why a lesson about online safety would refuse to collect anything about them.

        Closing prompts for a guest teacher.

        Nothing you did here was recorded.

        No account, no cookie, no analytics, no server. Close the tab and it is gone.

        Your five choices lived in one temporary list inside this page while you played. They were never sent anywhere, because this page has nothing to send them with.

        • No cookies. None are set, none are read.
        • No localStorage or sessionStorage. Nothing survives the tab closing.
        • No analytics, no tracking pixel, no third-party script.
        • No network requests beyond the page itself and a web font.
        • No sign-in, no name field, no free-text box — nothing to type your identity into.

        This is checkable, not just claimable: view the source of this page, or open the network tab and play it again. That verifiability is the whole thesis of PAIX. No profiling of individuals. Ever.

        What is wireframed, and what is real

        Real, working, in front of you

        • The loop — title, five scenarios in sequence, consequence, end screen, reset.
        • The choices and their consequence text, written to be argued with rather than graded.
        • The no-data guarantee. Verifiable by viewing source, not a promise in a policy.
        • The teacher view, with discussion prompts on every scenario.
        • Keyboard operation and a layout that holds at phone width.

        Not built yet — deliberately

        • Art. No illustration, no character design, no interface polish beyond the PAIX house style.
        • Audio. No voice, no music, no sound design.
        • More scenarios. Five is a demonstration; a term's worth is a different piece of work.
        • A proper accessibility audit — screen-reader testing, contrast verification, plain-language review.
        • Curriculum alignment to NASRO standards, and to whatever each district already teaches.

        Written this way on purpose. An SRO should be able to see exactly which parts are finished before anyone is asked to stand in front of a class with it.

        The Lantern Room is a PAIX educational wireframe, prepared 25 July 2026 for the Lenexa Police Department School Resource Unit. The department's own Police Department Structure page names guest teacher as the third basic function of an SRO, covering internet safety, bullying, violence prevention and de-escalation, and alcohol and drug prevention. This prototype addresses the first two of those.
