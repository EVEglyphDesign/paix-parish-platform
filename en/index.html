What this is

13 Catholic parishes, each with a mirrored site under a shared editorial template. Same header, same footer, same navigation — but each parish keeps its own crest, Mass times, contact information and diocesan link. If a parish already has an IT volunteer, this is not a replacement; it is an option they can inspect, fork, or ignore.

Locally hosted

Static site on GitHub Pages or the parish's own hosting. No third-party dashboard, no vendor lock-in.

No tracking

No cookies. No analytics. No third-party scripts other than Google Fonts. Marked noindex, nofollow until the parish approves publication.

Full parish ownership

The parish owns its content, its own domain name and its own exit. Fork the repository, take it home, and carry on.

Consistent design

A shared EVE Glyph Design canon, so parishioners moving between parish sites feel at home.

Nine languages, one baseline

This surface is not English with translations attached. Every text is first carried to a single Latin baseline, and every other language is rendered from that baseline. Latin is the pivot because it is the language the Church already holds in common, and because a pivot means no living language is subject to another living language.

Latin, English, French, Spanish, Italian, Portuguese, Romanian, Greek and Swahili. Greek is rendered from the Latin baseline just like every other language. Swahili is here because the platform is meant for all people, not only the wealthy — a person should be able to show this to their mother and sister in the language they actually think in, and be understood.

Proper names, street addresses, telephone numbers and clock times are never translated. They are rendered exactly as the parish publishes them, in every language.

For the Knights of Columbus

This is what scale within a week looks like — with no offense intended.

If the Knights want to make parish web presence uniform across a diocese, this template can bring up a new parish site within about an hour. But that is not what is being offered. What is offered is a choice:

  • The existing IT volunteer stays in place. If a Brother Knight or parishioner already runs the parish site, nothing is disturbed. This platform is available if they want to switch or fork it; otherwise it stands beside their existing work as a reference.
  • The money stays in the parish. No annual hosting fees, no website subscriptions, no advertising credits. Static hosting is free, and every dollar the parish saves stays in the community.
  • Parishioners stay safe. No tracking, no engagement-optimized feeds, no algorithmic exposure of children, women or the vulnerable. A parish site is a parish site — not a data-extraction surface for a remote platform.
  • The Knights remain in charge. Ownership, doctrine and exit rights belong to the parish and its Council. The design canon is a shared reference, not a contract with a vendor.

Council of Palms #6673 (Holy Trinity, Lenexa) is the reference council for this build.

The Charitable Ledger above is paired with the parish sites — that is the other half of what is offered.

A note — and who were the Acadians, anyway?

Down here at the bottom, because it is a note, not a sales pitch.

Long before all the parishes above, there was one at Port-Royal, in what is now Nova Scotia: Saint-Jean-Baptiste, founded 1613. Capuchin friars ran the mission from 1632. The first Catholic parish in what later became Canada.

Then most of the records ceased to exist. In 1654 a force from New England, led by Robert Sedgwick, took Port-Royal, killed the Capuchin superior Léonard de Chartres and expelled his fellow friars — against the terms of the capitulation they had just signed. Two registers survive: 1702–1728 and 1727–1755, now at the Centre acadien of Université Sainte-Anne. Everything before 1702 has perished.

The Greek card above is the other half of the same idea. In Nafplio, a wooden arch erected in 1841 carries the names of roughly 280 foreigners who died for someone else's homeland, each one with the place where he fell written beside it. Someone decided those names were worth keeping. In Port-Royal, someone decided ours were not.

People ask whether Rome kept a copy. Rome handles governance, not the sacraments — there is no central baptismal register in the Vatican. Baptisms are recorded in the parish, full stop. When the parish burns, that is the end of it.

One set, however, did make the journey. The registers of Saint-Charles-des-Mines from Grand-Pré went with the deported Acadians to Maryland, then to Louisiana, and were handed over to the priest at Fort San Gabriel in 1767. Today they sit in the vaults of the Diocese of Baton Rouge.

And no, this is not a claim that we are the oldest Catholic anything in North America. St. Augustine, Florida has the year 1565 — it is the second card at the top of this page. San Miguel Chapel in Santa Fe is around 1610. Port-Royal is only the first one that is ours, and the one whose records were burned.

The Congrès mondial acadien gathers the Acadian diaspora every five years. It is held by the Société Nationale de l'Acadie, and the 2029 congress will take place from 8 to 18 August in the Baie-des-Chaleurs, the region that includes Paquetville. Credit where it is due: the Acadian story belongs to that institution and to the people who carry it, not to this page. Anyone who wants the record from a legitimate source rather than from us should start there.
